# Exchange-Traded Funds (ETFs)

## What an ETF is
An exchange-traded fund pools money from many investors and invests it in a basket of assets —
stocks, bonds, or a mix. Buying one share of an ETF gives you a small slice of the whole basket,
which makes it an easy way to diversify.

## How ETFs differ from mutual funds
- **Trading:** ETF shares trade on stock exchanges throughout the day at market prices, like a
  stock. Mutual fund shares are bought and sold once a day at the fund's net asset value (NAV).
- **Price vs. value:** because an ETF trades on an exchange, its market price can be slightly above
  (a premium) or below (a discount) the value of its underlying holdings.
- **Minimums:** you can usually buy as little as one share (or a fraction, at some brokers).

## Potential benefits
- **Diversification** in a single purchase.
- **Low costs:** many ETFs, especially index ETFs, have low annual expense ratios.
- **Tax efficiency:** ETFs often distribute fewer capital gains than comparable mutual funds.

## Costs and risks to understand
- **Expense ratio:** the annual fee, taken as a percentage of your investment.
- **Bid-ask spread:** the small difference between buying and selling prices; wider for thinly
  traded ETFs.
- **Market risk:** an ETF can lose value just like the assets it holds.
- **Complex ETFs:** leveraged and inverse ETFs are designed for short-term trading and can behave very
  differently from what beginners expect over longer periods.

## Takeaway
Read an ETF's prospectus or summary to learn what it holds, what it costs, and what risks it carries.
