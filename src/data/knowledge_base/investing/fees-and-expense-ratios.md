# Investment Fees and Expense Ratios

## Why fees matter
Fees are one of the few things investors can control. They are taken out every year whether returns
are good or bad, and because they compound, small differences become large over decades.

## A worked example
Invest $10,000 for 20 years in two funds that each earn 6% a year before fees:
- **0.1% annual fee:** grows to about **$31,472**
- **1.0% annual fee:** grows to about **$26,533**

The 0.9-percentage-point fee difference costs almost **$5,000** — about half of the original
investment.

## Common fund fees
- **Expense ratio:** the fund's annual operating costs as a percentage of assets (e.g., 0.05%–1%+).
  Index funds typically have lower expense ratios than actively managed funds.
- **Sales loads:** commissions charged when buying (front-end) or selling (back-end) some mutual funds.
- **12b-1 fees:** ongoing marketing and distribution fees included in some funds' expenses.
- **Redemption or purchase fees** in some funds.

## Other costs to watch
- **Advisory fees** (for example, a percentage of assets per year for managed accounts).
- **Trading costs** such as bid-ask spreads on ETFs and stocks.
- **Account fees** charged by some brokers.

## How to compare
Look at the fee table in a fund's prospectus, and compare funds with similar strategies. A lower fee
doesn't guarantee a better fund, but a higher fee must be overcome by better performance just to
break even.
