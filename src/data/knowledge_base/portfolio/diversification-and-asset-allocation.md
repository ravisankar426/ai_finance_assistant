# Diversification and Asset Allocation

## Asset allocation
Asset allocation is how you divide your money among major asset categories — mainly **stocks,
bonds, and cash**. It is one of the most important decisions an investor makes because each
category behaves differently: stocks offer growth with larger swings, bonds offer income with
smaller swings, and cash offers stability with little growth.

The right mix depends mainly on your **time horizon** (when you need the money) and your **risk
tolerance** (how much decline you can live with). Longer horizons can usually accept more stocks.

## Diversification
Diversification means spreading money **within and across** asset categories so that a problem in
one investment doesn't sink your whole portfolio — "don't put all your eggs in one basket."
- **Within stocks:** many companies across different sectors and countries, not a few names.
- **Across asset classes:** stocks, bonds, and cash tend not to move in lockstep.

Diversification can reduce risk, but it **cannot guarantee a profit or prevent losses** in a broad
market decline.

## Concentration risk
Holding a large share of your portfolio in a single stock (including your employer's) or a single
sector increases the chance of a large loss. Mutual funds and ETFs are a simple way to diversify.

## Rebalancing
Over time, investments grow at different rates and your mix drifts from its target. Rebalancing
means bringing it back — for example, once a year or when an asset class drifts by more than a set
amount — by selling some of what grew and adding to what lagged, or by directing new contributions.
Be aware that selling in a taxable account can trigger capital-gains taxes.
