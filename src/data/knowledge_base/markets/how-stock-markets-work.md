# How Stock Markets Work

## Primary vs. secondary markets
Companies raise money by selling new shares to investors in the **primary market** — for example, in
an initial public offering (IPO). After that, investors buy and sell those shares among themselves in
the **secondary market**, on exchanges such as the New York Stock Exchange and Nasdaq, or through
other trading venues.

## What sets a stock's price
Prices are set by supply and demand: buyers' bids and sellers' asking prices. The **bid** is the
highest price a buyer will pay; the **ask** is the lowest a seller will accept; the gap is the
**bid-ask spread**. Prices move as investors react to company results, economic news, interest
rates, and sentiment.

## How your order gets filled
You place an order through a broker, who routes it to an exchange or another venue for execution.
Order types (market, limit, stop) control the price and timing.

## Trading hours
Major U.S. exchanges' regular session runs 9:30 a.m. to 4:00 p.m. Eastern Time on business days.
Some brokers offer extended-hours trading, which can have lower volume and wider spreads.

## Who oversees the markets
The SEC regulates the securities markets, and FINRA oversees broker-dealers. Exchanges also have
rules, including **circuit breakers** that pause trading during extreme market-wide declines.

## Why prices move day to day
In the short run, prices can swing for many reasons — some unrelated to a company's long-term value.
Over long periods, stock prices have tended to follow companies' earnings and growth.
