# Interest Rates and the Federal Reserve

## The Fed's role
The Federal Reserve is the U.S. central bank. Its monetary policy aims at **maximum employment and
stable prices**. Its main tool is setting a target range for the **federal funds rate** — the rate
banks charge each other for overnight loans — through the Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC).

## How rate changes spread
Changes in the federal funds rate ripple through the economy: rates on savings accounts, CDs, credit
cards, auto loans, and (indirectly) mortgages tend to move in the same direction.
- **Raising rates** cools borrowing and spending to fight inflation.
- **Cutting rates** encourages borrowing and spending to support the economy.

## Effects on investments
- **Bonds:** when rates rise, existing bond prices fall (and vice versa); longer-term bonds are more
  sensitive.
- **Stocks:** higher rates raise companies' borrowing costs and make safer assets relatively more
  attractive, which can weigh on stock prices — especially fast-growing companies whose profits are
  far in the future.
- **Cash:** savers earn more when rates are higher.

## Why markets watch the Fed
Investors react not only to rate decisions but to signals about **future** decisions. Markets often
move ahead of the actual change.

## Keep in mind
Rate moves are just one influence among many; predicting markets from Fed decisions is unreliable.
