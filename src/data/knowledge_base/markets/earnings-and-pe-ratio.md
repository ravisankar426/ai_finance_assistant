# Earnings and the P/E Ratio

## Earnings
A company's earnings are its profits. Public companies report results every quarter, including
revenue and **earnings per share (EPS)** — net income divided by shares outstanding. Stock prices
often react to whether results beat or miss analysts' expectations.

## The price-earnings (P/E) ratio
P/E = share price ÷ earnings per share. A stock at $100 with $5 of annual EPS has a P/E of 20 —
investors are paying $20 for each $1 of yearly earnings.
- **Trailing P/E** uses the past 12 months' earnings.
- **Forward P/E** uses analysts' estimates of future earnings.

## Interpreting P/E
- A **higher P/E** often reflects expectations of faster growth — or optimism that may not be met.
- A **lower P/E** may signal a bargain, slower growth, or problems.
- P/E is most meaningful when comparing similar companies or a company with its own history; it
  isn't comparable across very different industries.
- Companies with losses have no meaningful P/E.

## Where to find reports
Companies file annual reports (Form 10-K) and quarterly reports (Form 10-Q) with the SEC, available
free on EDGAR.

## Caution
No single ratio tells you whether a stock is a good investment; valuation is one piece alongside the
business, its risks, and your diversification.
