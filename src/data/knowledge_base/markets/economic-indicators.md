# Key Economic Indicators

## Gross Domestic Product (GDP)
GDP measures the value of the final goods and services produced in the U.S. and is the broadest
measure of economic activity. The Bureau of Economic Analysis (BEA) releases it quarterly, usually
as an annualized growth rate adjusted for inflation ("real GDP"). Two consecutive quarters of
decline are often discussed informally as a sign of recession, though official U.S. recession dating
uses a broader set of measures.

## Inflation (CPI)
The Consumer Price Index from the Bureau of Labor Statistics tracks the average change in consumer
prices. It influences Federal Reserve policy, interest rates, and inflation-adjusted benefits like
Social Security.

## Jobs and unemployment
The monthly employment report shows jobs added and the unemployment rate — key signals of economic
strength and of possible Fed action.

## Interest rates and the yield curve
Treasury yields across maturities form the **yield curve**. When short-term yields rise above
long-term yields (an "inverted" curve), it has historically been watched as a possible recession
warning, though not a perfect one.

## How investors use indicators
Indicators describe the economy, and markets react when results differ from expectations. But stock
markets often move ahead of the economy, so indicators are better for understanding context than
for timing trades.
