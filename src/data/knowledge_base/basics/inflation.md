# Inflation and Purchasing Power

## What inflation is
Inflation is the general rise in prices over time. When prices rise, each dollar buys less — your
money loses **purchasing power**. In the U.S., the most widely followed measure is the **Consumer
Price Index (CPI)**, published monthly by the Bureau of Labor Statistics. It tracks the average
change in prices paid by consumers for a representative basket of goods and services such as food,
housing, transportation, and medical care.

## Why it matters for savers and investors
At 3% annual inflation, $100 today buys only about **$74** worth of today's goods after 10 years, and
prices roughly double in about **24 years** (rule of 72: 72 ÷ 3). Cash that earns less than
inflation is losing real value even though the dollar balance doesn't shrink.

## Nominal vs. real returns
- **Nominal return:** the percentage your investment grew.
- **Real return:** roughly the nominal return minus inflation. A 5% return during 3% inflation is
  about a 2% real gain.

Long-term goals such as retirement are usually planned in real (inflation-adjusted) terms, because
what matters is what your money will buy.

## Protecting against inflation
- Stocks have historically outpaced inflation over long periods, with significant short-term risk.
- Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities (TIPS) and Series I savings bonds adjust for inflation.
- Holding large amounts of cash for many years tends to lose ground to inflation.

## Keep in mind
The CPI reflects an average household; your personal inflation rate depends on what you actually buy.
