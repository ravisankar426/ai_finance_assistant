# Risk and Return

## The basic trade-off
In investing, **risk** is the possibility that an investment's actual return will differ from what
you expect — including the possibility of losing money. **Return** is what you gain or lose. As a
general rule, investments with the potential for higher returns carry higher risk. No investment
offers high returns with no risk; a promise of that is a warning sign of fraud.

## Rough risk ladder (lowest to highest, generally)
1. **Cash and insured savings accounts** — very low risk of loss, but returns may not keep up with
   inflation.
2. **Government bonds** — low default risk, but prices move with interest rates.
3. **Corporate bonds** — more credit risk, higher expected return.
4. **Stocks** — the highest long-term expected return of the three main asset classes, with the
   largest short-term swings.

## Kinds of risk
- **Market risk:** the whole market falls, taking most investments with it.
- **Inflation risk:** your money grows more slowly than prices rise, so you lose purchasing power.
- **Concentration risk:** too much money in one company, sector, or asset.
- **Liquidity risk:** you can't sell quickly without accepting a lower price.

## Time horizon matters
The longer you can leave money invested, the more time it has to recover from downturns. Money you
need within a few years is usually kept in lower-risk places, while long-term goals like retirement
can typically tolerate more stock-market risk.

## Risk tolerance
Your risk tolerance is how much loss you can accept — financially and emotionally — without
abandoning your plan. Selling in a panic after a drop can turn a temporary loss into a permanent one.
