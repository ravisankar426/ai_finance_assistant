# Compound Interest

## What compounding means
Compound interest is earning returns on your previous returns, not just on the money you originally
invested. Each period, earnings are added to your balance, and the next period's earnings are
calculated on that larger balance. Over long periods this snowball effect can become the largest
source of growth.

## A worked example
Invest $1,000 at a 5% annual return, compounded once a year:
- After 1 year: $1,000 × 1.05 = **$1,050**
- After 10 years: $1,000 × 1.05^10 ≈ **$1,629**
- After 30 years: $1,000 × 1.05^30 ≈ **$4,322**

Simple interest (earning 5% only on the original $1,000) would give $1,500 after 10 years and $2,500
after 30 years. The difference is entirely the effect of compounding.

## The formula
Future value = P × (1 + r)^n, where P is the starting amount, r is the return per period, and n is
the number of periods. Adding regular contributions makes the effect even stronger.

## The rule of 72
A quick estimate of how long money takes to double: divide 72 by the annual return in percent.
At 6% a year, money roughly doubles in 72 ÷ 6 = **12 years**.

## Why starting early matters
Because growth builds on itself, time is the most powerful input. Someone who starts investing
earlier can end up with more than someone who invests more later.

## Important caveats
Investment returns are not guaranteed and vary from year to year; a steady 5% is a simplification.
Compounding also works against you with debt: unpaid credit-card interest compounds too.
